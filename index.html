The short version

The agent is doing the engineering but too few engineers are directing it.

The assessment grades what the engineer delivered separately from how they ran the AI agent that helped them deliver it. Those two scores should move together, but across this cohort they barely do (correlation ).

Output is healthy, with the median engineer scoring on the full-stack build, on the AI-systems diagnosis and on the production debugging task. A leadership dashboard that tracks only shipped work would call this team ready.

Direction is not, with a median score of out of 100 for how engineers steered the agent and of engineers scoring below 50. Most did not ask the agent to explore the codebase before it built, did not push back on its plan, and did not demand proof that the result worked.

Strong output with weak direction is a risk that doesn’t show up yet. It holds until the codebase, the requirement or the model changes. Then nobody in the loop is checking the agent.

The good news is that steering is a habit, not a talent. It is concentrated in a small set of behaviours (orient, plan, parallelize, verify) that can be taught. This report shows who already has them, where the gaps sit by team, and what it would take to close them.

What was assessed

Four scenarios, two kinds of evidence

Each engineer worked through four timed, real-world scenarios with their own AI coding agent switched on. Blind human graders scored the results against a binary rubric of more than a hundred checks.

The platform rolls the four scores into one blended score and places each engineer in one of three bands, Ready (75 and above), Developing (40 to 74) or Foundational (below 40). Across this cohort, engineers are Ready, are Developing and are Foundational. The median blended score is .

The blended score is a fair summary, but it hides the most useful fact in the data. The assessment allows AI throughout, so three of the four scores partly measure the agent. Only the Harness score isolates the engineer’s own contribution. That is why this report keeps direction and output apart.

Finding 01

Output looks healthy. Direction doesn’t.

On the three output scenarios, seven in ten scores land between 55 and 95. On direction, the cohort piles up near the bottom of the scale.

Why the two diverge

The assessment is designed to allow AI. A capable agent given a clear brief will produce a working approval gate, a reasonable audit trail and a plausible root-cause list on its own. The output rubric credits those results whether or not the engineer shaped them. The Harness rubric credits only what the engineer did, meaning the instructions, corrections and checks they gave the agent.

So the gap between the two is a rough measure of how much of the output is the agent’s work. Across the cohort the median engineer’s output score sits points above their direction score.

Finding 03

The gaps sit in four habits of directing an agent

Engineers pass about three output checks in four, but only about one direction check in three. Just one of the eighteen direction checks, prioritizing under the clock, was passed by more than half the cohort.

What the rarest behaviours have in common

The four behaviours seen in a quarter or fewer of engineers are parallelizing work, using their own tooling, catching the agent out, and asking for a gap analysis. All four treat the agent as something to manage rather than something to type at. They are also the behaviours that multiply output on real work, because parallel agents shorten delivery and catching a faked test stops a defect before review.

Setup friction is the cheapest problem in this report to fix, and it is not a skill problem. A third of all prompts in the cohort (%) went to getting the agent access to the repository. That points to a developer-environment gap, where agents lack pre-authorised Git access, repositories lack agent instructions, and local permission profiles block the tools the agent needs.

One session, up close

What a Passenger’s 47 minutes actually look like

This is one of the real assessment records the cohort was modelled on. The engineer’s output scored well, but their direction score was 5.

What the graders saw

The first 18 minutes went on cloning the repository and signing in to GitHub. The engineer then pasted the brief a second time and asked “you have all the requirement with you?”. Three of the next five prompts were countdowns (“we have 9 mins”, “6 mins left”, “only 3 mins”).

The agent still delivered a working approval gate and a complete audit trail, so the build scored 70. The Harness rubric found one habit out of eighteen, prioritizing under the clock. The agent was never asked to explore the code, its plan was never questioned, and nothing it claimed was checked.

The perfect score

On the RAG incident the engineer scored 100, the highest possible, and the session log shows how. They pasted the brief, asked the agent for root causes, asked it to “reevaluate again with far more deeper thoughts”, then asked “so what to do, copy paste as answers?”.

The answer was excellent, but it was also the agent’s answer, and the graders flagged it as copy-pasted AI output with minimal editing. On paper this engineer is a top RAG diagnostician, but in practice nobody yet knows.
